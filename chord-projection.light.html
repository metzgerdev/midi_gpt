How a chord becomes a vector

Where the token lookup picks one row, the chord projection adds rows. A minor lights three pitch classes, so the layer sums their three learned vectors and adds a bias. Real weights from the trained bass checkpoint.

The arithmetic

chord_proj(A minor) = W[:,C] + W[:,E] + W[:,A] + b

Verified against the checkpoint: linear(x) and the hand-summed columns are identical to within 1e-6. The general form is a weighted sum of all twelve columns, but a binary chroma makes nine of the weights zero, so the multiply degenerates into a selection-and-add. Two consequences follow. The bias is added for every chord, including an all-zero chroma — "no chord" is not the zero vector. And gradients are sparse in the same way a lookup's are: with x = 0 the gradient for that column is zero, so only C, E and A move on this step. The bias moves every step.

But that is mostly arithmetic, not learning

The ordering is clean — 2/3 shared beats 1/3 beats 0/3 — and it matches music theory: C major is A minor's relative major, and scores highest.

It would be a mistake to read that as the model having learned harmony. Sums that share two of three terms are mechanically similar, and every chord also carries the same bias, which floors the similarity well above zero — even chords sharing no notes sit at +0.24 to +0.33.

What is genuinely learned is the spread within a group: C major at +0.724 against F major at +0.665, both sharing two notes. That gap comes from the vectors themselves. The gross ordering comes from the encoding.
